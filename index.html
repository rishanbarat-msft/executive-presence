HABITS OF READINESS

Six things to do before the room opens.

READ EVERYTHING
The most prepared person rarely has to be the loudest. Skim broadly. Read deeply.
DEFINE YOUR GOALS
Name three outcomes you need by the end. Without goals, the conversation drifts.
START WITH THE BASICS
Concise, direct questions outperform clever ones. Let the question do the work.
IMAGINE THE DIALOGUE
Walk the conversation through in your head. Anticipate the pushback.
WHAT THEY SEE IS WHAT THEY BELIEVE

THE FOUR MOVES

How to lead a room when no one has the answer.

01
ADMIT WHAT YOU DON’T KNOW
Misinformation thrives in a vacuum. Naming uncertainty up front protects trust. Promise an update — and deliver it.
02
STAY A PROFESSIONAL
Bring clarity, analysis, and perspective. Give people a frame they can act on, not a performance.
03
STAY A HUMAN
Acknowledge what people are feeling in the room. Empathy is not a soft skill — it’s how you keep an audience with you.
04
FOCUS ON KEY QUESTIONS
Anticipate the three biggest questions your audience has, and answer those first. Everything else can wait.
“Courage is not the absence of fear, but rather the judgment that something else is more important.”— Ambrose Redmoon

PRINCIPLES TO CARRY WITH YOU

Six final lessons.

ON HONESTY
Lead with your weaknesses. People connect with vulnerability before they trust authority.
ON SILENCE
The pauses you allow can carry more weight than the words you choose.
ON HARD NEWS
Stay calm. Stay contained. But stay human. That balance is the whole job.
ON TOUGH QUESTIONS
Don’t make it personal. Compare actions to commitments. If done with respect, it lands.
ON OBJECTIVITY
Give people your insight, your analysis — not your conclusion. Let them get there.
ON GREAT CONVERSATIONS
Three signals: time disappears, people finish each other’s sentences, and there is laughter.
“The art of communication is the language of leadership.”— James Humes
